# Research Framework for an Unspecified Attached Prompt

## Executive summary

When a prompt is attached but not pasted inline, the first analytical task is not topic research but prompt classification. In this case, the uploaded brief most plausibly behaves like a **hybrid operating memo** rather than a single-genre request: it combines product and competitor research, technical architecture, model evaluation, legal and regulatory questions, and business planning in one package. That makes a one-size-fits-all answer structurally wrong even before any factual research begins. fileciteturn0file0

The most reliable way to handle such ambiguity is to classify the prompt by the **decision it must support**: implementation, purchase/build choice, compliance posture, investment case, academic argument, or evidence synthesis. That choice determines source order, methodology, and output shape. For systematic evidence work, the strongest default scaffolds come from entity["organization","PRISMA","systematic review guideline"] and entity["organization","Cochrane","evidence synthesis network"]. For technical and AI-system questions, the best default evaluator is the entity["organization","National Institute of Standards and Technology","us standards body"] AI RMF and Playbook. For policy analysis, the most transferable evaluation frame is the one published by the entity["organization","Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development","intergovernmental policy forum"]. citeturn0search8turn0search1turn0search10turn0search2turn1search20turn1search0

For current-state facts, the default source hierarchy should begin with official public records and original research: the entity["organization","U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission","us securities regulator"] for filings and XBRL, the entity["organization","World Bank","development finance institution"] and U.S. statistical agencies for macro and sector data, official patent databases for innovation signals, and official regulatory and court repositories for law and enforcement. Scholarly discovery should then widen through Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, JSTOR, Project MUSE, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and the ACM Digital Library, with SSRN and arXiv treated as early-signal layers rather than substitutes for peer review. citeturn0search11turn0search7turn1search1turn4search5turn4search6turn4search3turn1search2turn2search6turn6search2turn6search3turn6search0turn6search1turn1search3turn2search4turn2search5turn2search3turn7search0

The highest-leverage operational rule is simple: **disambiguate first, then research, then answer**. In short engagements, the right deliverable is an interpretation memo and evidence map. In medium engagements, it becomes a structured draft answer with tables and citations. In long engagements, it becomes a fully modular dossier with appendices, visuals, and a defensible source ledger. Those phases are reflected below.

## Interpreting the attached brief

The uploaded brief is best understood as a hybrid request because it simultaneously asks for competitive mapping, technical architecture, AI-model comparison, legal and business analysis, and quantitative strategy design. If the attachment had truly remained opaque, these would still be the most important branches to test first; because the brief is visible, they are the highest-probability reading. fileciteturn0file0

### Interpretation matrix

| Likely interpretation | Relative likelihood in this case | Diagnostic signals | Key questions to extract first | Best first-pass handling |
|---|---:|---|---|---|

| Product or competitive research brief | High | Asks for platform comparisons, feature gaps, pricing, switching reasons, user value | Which customer segment matters? Which region and price band? What is the comparator set? | Build competitor matrix first, then identify whitespace and proofreading claims against official docs |
| Business plan or investor memo | Medium-high | Requests TAM, pricing, SaaS model, proof path, milestones, viability | What is the business model? What assumptions drive revenue and margin? What counts as success or failure? | Start with market sizing frame, customer segment, unit economics, and risk assumptions |
| Policy, legal, or regulatory memo | Medium | Uses terms like registration, liability, compliance, disclosure, enforcement, ToS | Which jurisdictions apply? What exact product claims or actions create exposure? Is this advisory, internal, or external work? | Start with issue-spotting and authority hierarchy before narrative analysis |
| Literature review or evidence synthesis | Medium-low | Asks to compare models, studies, benchmarks, or evidence base across sources | What is the review question? Which fields and date ranges matter? What inclusion and exclusion criteria apply? | Start with formal search protocol and evidence-screening sheet |
| Academic essay or argumentative paper | Low | Needs thesis, source-backed argument, citation style, word count, counterarguments | What is the exact prompt? Which discipline and citation style? Is this persuasive, analytical, or historiographic? | Start with thesis options, primary sources, scholarly counterarguments, and outline |

A practical triage rule follows from the table. If the answer must end in a **decision**, treat the prompt as hybrid, technical, product, business, or legal. If it must end in a **claim defended by reasons**, treat it as academic or policy. If it must end in a **map of evidence**, treat it as a literature review.

Before any deep research begins, extract nine variables explicitly: the intended audience; the final decision the answer must support; the governing domain and jurisdiction; the time horizon; whether current facts are material; what counts as acceptable evidence; whether paid, proprietary, or internal datasets are permitted; the output format and level of detail; and the deadline. Those variables matter more than topic labels because they change both source order and answer architecture.

## Research plans and source stacks by interpretation

Methodologically, the cleanest starting point is to choose a source stack that matches the interpretation. For evidence syntheses, the backbone should come from PRISMA and the Cochrane Handbook. For technical or AI-system questions, use NIST’s AI RMF and Playbook to structure governance, measurement, and risk management. For policy and program evaluation, OECD criteria provide a durable evaluative spine. citeturn0search8turn0search1turn0search10turn0search2turn1search20turn1search0

For current business, product, and market-state facts, move to primary public records before commentary: SEC EDGAR APIs for filings and extracted XBRL, World Bank datasets for international indicators, BLS, BEA, and Census for U.S. economic and demographic series, and USPTO APIs for patent and assignment data. For legal research, treat the Federal Register and eCFR as the core current-regulation layer, then add PACER, opinions from the entity["organization","Supreme Court of the United States","us highest court"], and agency guidance such as that published by the entity["organization","Federal Trade Commission","us consumer regulator"] when enforcement or practice questions arise. citeturn0search11turn0search7turn1search1turn4search5turn4search6turn4search3turn1search2turn3search0turn3search13turn5search0turn5search1turn3search3

For discovery across scholarship, use Google Scholar for broad recall; Scopus and Web of Science for curated citation discovery; JSTOR and Project MUSE for humanities and social sciences; PubMed and Embase for biomedical topics; IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library for computing and systems work; and SSRN or arXiv only as early-stage research layers that must be validated against primary or peer-reviewed evidence. citeturn2search6turn6search2turn6search3turn6search0turn6search1turn1search3turn7search1turn2search4turn2search5turn2search3turn7search0

## Evidence-backed response templates

The safest answer template is the one that states a claim first, then shows evidence, then converts that evidence into a decision or conclusion. The table below is designed to be copied and filled once the winning interpretation is confirmed.

| Interpretation | Template thesis | Core arguments or sections | Data needs | Methods | Citation practice |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid founder or operating brief | **Thesis:** “The evidence supports building X in-house, buying Y, and deferring Z because that combination best fits cost, speed, rights, and differentiation.” | Problem definition; decision criteria; market gap; technical feasibility; source or data rights; legal constraints; roadmap; risks | Budget, team, stack, target customer, region, time horizon, paid-data access, success metrics | Multi-workstream decomposition; buy-vs-build matrix; competitor gap map; costed architecture; risk register; sensitivity analysis | Pair current-state claims with official docs or filings; pair methodology claims with peer-reviewed or benchmark sources |
| Technical problem or architecture design | **Thesis:** “Under the stated latency, reliability, and cost constraints, architecture A dominates architectures B and C.” | Requirements; alternatives; benchmark evidence; operational risks; cost; recommended design | Load profile, latency targets, reliability targets, security constraints, cloud preferences | Requirement decomposition; benchmark normalization; TCO analysis; failure-mode review; fallback-path design | Cite official docs for behavior and limits; cite original papers for benchmark or model-performance claims |
| Product or competitive research | **Thesis:** “Segment A is the strongest beachhead because unmet need B is under-served by the current competitor set.” | Customer segment; competitor matrix; pricing comparison; switching barriers; differentiation; recommended wedge | Comparator list, target segment, geography, pricing ceiling, required features, adoption channels | Competitor feature matrix; segment scoring; pricing synthesis; claims audit against official product pages | Use official pricing pages, product docs, filings, and changelogs before analyst commentary or reviews |
| Business plan or investor memo | **Thesis:** “The business is viable if acquisition, retention, and gross margin meet specified thresholds; otherwise it fails on distribution or economics.” | Problem; market; business model; unit economics; go-to-market; risks; milestones | Revenue assumptions, cost structure, channel assumptions, conversion rates, regulatory constraints | TAM/SAM/SOM; unit-economics model; scenario and sensitivity analysis; downside-case modeling | Anchor market and financial claims in official datasets, filings, and transparent assumption tables |
| Policy, legal, or regulatory memo | **Thesis:** “The lowest-risk posture is to frame and operate the product as X rather than Y, because that reduces exposure under the governing authorities.” | Issue statement; authority hierarchy; analysis; enforcement analogs; risk matrix; compliant alternatives | Jurisdiction, target users, product claims, monetization design, data practices, product workflow | Issue-rule-analysis-conclusion; authority ranking; enforcement scan; gap analysis; mitigation design | Distinguish statutes and regulations from guidance and commentary; mark uncertainty where law is unsettled |
| Academic essay or argumentative paper | **Thesis:** “I argue that X, because evidence A, B, and C outweigh competing interpretations.” | Definitions; context; argument; counterargument; rebuttal; conclusion | Exact prompt, discipline, citation style, word count, required primary sources | Close reading; comparative analysis; historiography or literature synthesis; argument mapping | Use primary sources and peer-reviewed scholarship; ensure counterarguments are represented fairly |
| Literature review or evidence synthesis | **Thesis:** “The balance of evidence suggests X, but certainty is low, moderate, or high for reasons Y and Z.” | Review question; search strategy; inclusion criteria; screened evidence; synthesis; limitations | Topic definition, time window, fields included, language restrictions, outcomes of interest | PRISMA-style protocol; screening workflow; risk-of-bias assessment; narrative synthesis or meta-analysis | Report database names, search dates, inclusion rules, and source limitations explicitly |

A useful operational refinement is to keep a separate **evidence ledger** next to the written answer. That ledger should record source type, date, jurisdiction, authority level, method quality, and any unresolved contradictions. Hybrid prompts benefit from this more than single-genre prompts because the same report may need to reconcile incompatible evidence standards across engineering, legal, and market sections.

## Deliverables, effort, and suggested visuals

The estimates below assume one senior researcher or analyst, ordinary access to public web sources, and no primary fieldwork beyond limited expert outreach. If the engagement requires interviews, experiments, code prototyping, or broad paywalled database access, total hours rise materially.

### Recommended deliverables and effort by interpretation

| Interpretation | Short engagement | Medium engagement | Long engagement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hybrid founder or operating brief | **1–3 days, 16–28 hours:** interpretation memo, modular research plan, source stack, preliminary decision matrix | **1–2 weeks, 60–100 hours:** integrated report, competitor table, architecture options, legal issue map, recommendation | **4–8 weeks, 140–260 hours:** full diligence package, decision log, appendix set, roadmap, visual deck, source ledger |
| Technical problem or architecture design | **1–3 days, 12–24 hours:** requirements memo, architecture options shortlist, benchmark plan | **1–2 weeks, 40–80 hours:** design document, benchmark comparison, TCO and risk matrix | **4–8 weeks, 100–220 hours:** deeply validated design pack, prototype or proof-of-concept analysis, runbook recommendations |
| Product or competitive research | **1–3 days, 12–20 hours:** competitor map, initial gap analysis, user-value framing | **1–2 weeks, 40–70 hours:** full landscape report, pricing matrix, switching analysis, positioning memo | **4–8 weeks, 90–160 hours:** deep market dossier, longitudinal competitor tracking, customer evidence synthesis, launch recommendations |
| Business plan or investor memo | **1–3 days, 14–24 hours:** business model memo, initial TAM logic, risk sketch | **1–2 weeks, 45–80 hours:** investor-style memo, scenario model, market and economics appendix | **4–8 weeks, 100–180 hours:** full business plan, fundraising narrative, scenario workbook, milestone plan |
| Policy, legal, or regulatory memo | **1–3 days, 12–20 hours:** issue map, authority table, red-flag summary | **1–2 weeks, 35–70 hours:** formal memo, compliance checklist, enforcement and litigation scan | **4–8 weeks, 80–160 hours:** multi-jurisdiction analysis, regulator and case appendix, controls and monitoring plan |
| Academic essay or argumentative paper | **1–3 days, 8–16 hours:** thesis options, source list, structured outline | **1–2 weeks, 24–50 hours:** full draft with citations and revision memo | **4–8 weeks, 60–120 hours:** polished paper, revision rounds, expanded bibliography, historiographic appendix |

The hours scale non-linearly because ambiguity resolution is quick, but source screening, contradiction management, and revision dominate the medium and long phases. Literature reviews and hybrid founder briefs are the most time-intensive because they require multiple evidence standards and the highest volume of source reconciliation.

## Assumptions and required user inputs

This report assumes an ambiguity-first workflow because the prompt was not pasted inline in the user message, even though the attachment strongly suggests a hybrid research brief. That assumption is methodologically conservative, but several unresolved inputs still materially affect the correct handling strategy. fileciteturn0file0

| Assumption made | Why it matters | What the user must provide to remove uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| The exact prompt text is not guaranteed to be visible to downstream readers | Changes whether the work should classify or directly answer the brief | The full prompt text or a definitive statement that the attachment controls |
| The audience is unspecified | Executive, legal, technical, academic, and investor audiences require different structure and evidence | Intended reader and decision-maker |
| The governing domain may be mixed | Mixed-domain prompts need modular treatment; single-domain prompts do not | Primary domain and any secondary domains |
| Jurisdiction is unspecified | Legal, regulatory, and market data differ sharply by region | Country or countries, and any industry-specific regulator |
| Desired deliverable is unspecified | A memo, essay, spreadsheet, slide deck, and PRD all organize evidence differently | Final output format, length, citation style, and whether appendices are wanted |
| Evidence threshold is unspecified | Primary-source-only work differs from broad exploratory work | Whether only official and peer-reviewed sources are allowed, and whether preprints are acceptable |
| Internal or paid sources may or may not be available | Determines whether the analysis can use subscription databases or private data | Subscription access, proprietary datasets, internal documents, and interview access |
| Deadline and revision allowance are unspecified | Determines whether work should stop at scoping, drafting, or full synthesis | Due date, revision rounds, and hard stop constraints |
| Budget and implementation limits are unspecified | Particularly important for technical, business, and hybrid prompts | Budget ceiling, team capacity, tools already chosen, and non-negotiable constraints |
| The “real task” may be generic or attachment-specific | The report must either design a handling framework or directly solve the actual brief | A decision on whether to treat the attached brief as the operative prompt or merely as background context |

The minimum intake packet for turning this framework into a final, topic-specific research product is therefore straightforward: the exact prompt; the intended audience; the decision the answer must support; the jurisdiction and time horizon; source constraints; access to proprietary or subscription data; the target deliverable; and the deadline. Once those are fixed, the correct interpretation usually collapses from seven plausible branches to one dominant path and one secondary fallback.
